past_projects:
  - title: Comparative and evolutionary genomics of ticks
    text: |-
      I was a key contributor to a comparative genomics project generating chromosome-level assemblies for four *Ixodes* tick species, including *I. ricinus*, the main vector of Lyme disease, and using them to study tick genome architecture and long-term evolutionary history — a foundational piece of work that gave the field genomic resources for this genus. Building on that work, I turned to horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from bacteria into tick genomes. I identified several genes originating from HGTs. Among them, a bacterial gene (AnmK), normally involved in recycling bacterial cell-wall material, was acquired by the common ancestor of all ticks and has since become functionally integrated into tick biology.

  - title: Evolution of viral domestication in Campopleginae wasps
    text: |-
      During my PhD, I studied how parasitoid wasps domesticate viruses, turning them into weapons against the insects they parasitize. Working mainly on Campopleginae wasps like *Venturia canescens*, I showed that conserved, domesticated viral transcriptional machinery drives the production of virus-like particles (VLPs), which help wasp larvae suppress their host's immune defenses. I described two other endogenous nudiviruses, one endogenised in *Campoplex capitator*, the other one endogenised in *Campoplex nolae*, and traced how this nudivirus-derived VLP system evolved in this group of wasps. In a comparative follow-up, I found that unrelated domesticated viral lineages — nudivirus-derived VLPs in *Campoplex* wasps and ichnoviruses — had independently and convergently recruited the same proteins (transcribed from *repeat element* genes), suggesting there's a shared evolutionary "toolkit" that different wasp lineages draw on when domesticating viruses.

interests: |-
  My work focuses on the evolution of organisms, especially the evolution of arthropod genomes.

  I use genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic data to find evolutionary patterns among species. These patterns can take several forms, from single nucleotide polymorphisms to chromosome structural changes — they help me better understand the link between genome and phenotype, and to draw evolutionary relationships between organisms. In combination with phenotypic features, genomic features can also be used to predict species life-history traits and evaluate species susceptibility to, for example, environmental change.

background_text: |-
  I am a French researcher specialising in evolutionary biology and bioinformatics. I earned my PhD in molecular biology at the University of Tours (France), supervised by Prof Huguet and Dr Josse.

  My research background focuses on evolutionary biology, leveraging genomic data to uncover insights into the evolutionary processes shaping organisms. During my PhD I explored the evolutionary dynamics of viral symbiosis in parasitoid wasps, providing insights into the interactions between wasp and viral genomes and their impact on wasp biology. Afterwards, through comparative genomics, I studied tick genome evolution, which highlighted features such as gene expansions that support their parasitic lifestyle. Beyond my primary research projects, I've shared my bioinformatics expertise with students and researchers, contributing to multiple publications, and taught genetics and basic evolution concepts to undergraduates.

  My study approaches integrate molecular and evolutionary biology, bioinformatics, and functional and comparative genomics.
